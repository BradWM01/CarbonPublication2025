\begin{abstract}

Nuclear grade graphite acts as a structural component and moderator in the
remaining advanced gas-cooled nuclear reactors (AGRs) in the UK, and in several
next generation (Generation IV) reactor designs.  Its microstructure, in
particular porosity, dictates material properties and their evolution under
operational conditions. We present a novel approach to characterising such
microstructure, by inverse modelling of channel (surface visible) porosity
estimated from the image analysis of electron micrographs, coupled with
previously measured mercury intrusion porosimetry and surface adsorption
converted to void size by grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations. The result is
a void network comprising pores over the range 0.4 nm to 115 µm, a much larger
range than can be obtained from X-Ray computed tomographic (CT) scanning. The
method was validated by comparing the simulated open porosities of the void
network for nuclear graphite grades IG-110 and IG-430 to the open porosities
quoted by their manufacturer Toyo Tanso.  The estimates are within 0.56\% and
0.71\% of the manufacturer's measurements. Once the void network has been
generated, a range of further simulations of pore fluid behaviour can be carried
out.  We illustrate this by displaying the extent of intrusion of molten salt
into IG-110 that would occur at high temperature in a Molten Salt Reactor.  The
new method has very wide applicability, as it can be used for any rigid porous
material which has been studied by microscopy and porosimetry, but for which
pycnometry is unavailable or inappropriate.

\section{Introduction}
Graphite is employed as a moderator, neutron reflector and structural component
in the remaining advanced gas-cooled nuclear reactors (AGRs) in the UK, and in
several Generation IV reactor designs, including the High Temperature Gas
Reactor (HTGR) and Molten Salt Reactor (MSR) \citep{international2025iaea,
international2023iaeaMSRs, MARSDENgeniv}. Nuclear grade graphite  is
characterised by exceptionally low boron content, high structural strength,
thermal stability, and a high scattering and low absorbent neutron cross-section
\citep{Marsden2016}. 

The internal microstructure of nuclear grade graphite plays a key role in its
mechanical and thermo-physical properties, and the evolution under irradiation
of these properties and thermo-chemical properties such as oxidation, gas
release and thermal degradation \citep{MARSDENgeniv, ARREGUIMENA2022112047}. For
HTGRs, oxidation rates are relevant in fault conditions involving air ingress,
and are typically higher where open porosity is distributed uniformly
\citep{PAUL2022132,GraphitePIRT}. Additionally, porosity strongly affects
gaseous diffusion, in turn influencing degradation rate, determining whether the
behaviour under oxidative conditions will be diffusion-controlled or
kinetic-controlled \citep{KANE2018369, MATTHEWS2021111245, PAUL2022132}.
Impedance of diffusion of gaseous fission products into the graphite bulk
requires a He permeability of less than $1 \times
10^{-8}~\text{cm}^2\text{s}^{-1}~\text{cm}$, corresponding to pore diameters of
100 nm or less\citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt, SONG201436}. In MSRs, unwanted salt
intrusion can occur into surface pores greater than 1 µm in diameter
\citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt}. 



\subsection{Experimental techniques}
\label{subsec:Experimental techniques}
The primary experimental techniques for microstructural characterisation are
mercury intrusion porosimetry, helium pycnometry, gas adsorption, X-ray computed
tomography (X-ray CT), and microscopy. Mercury intrusion porosimetry is an
established technique for characterising the pore size distribution of nuclear
grade graphite \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047, JONES2020256HgHe, CONTESCU2019663,
Jones2020}. However, the technique destroys the voids structure at the highest
applied pressures of mercury necessary to probe voids below 100 nm in size, is
destructive, and unable to characterise the very smallest pores as the pressure
required induces damage in the microstructure. Helium pycnometry by contrast is
a non-destructive technique that measures the total open porosity of a sample,
but does not provide information on the pore size distribution. Gas adsorption
techniques, such as nitrogen or krypton adsorption, are also non-destructive and
provide detailed characterisation of the pore size distribution in the nanometre
range.

X-ray CT is a non-destructive technique that has also been used to characterise
the microstructure of nuclear grade graphite
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047,KANE2020291,Liu2017, MOSTAFAVI2013410,
BARHLI2017357}. Recent X-ray CT characterisation efforts relevant to MSR and
HTGR operation include real-time tracking of salt infiltration and modelling of
the relation between microstructure and oxidation rate \citep{ZHANG2024102016,
PAUL2022132}. However, X-ray CT has limited spatial resolution, being typically
unable to resolves pores with volumes smaller than 10 µm³
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047}.  

Those studies which have attempted to characterise the surface porosity of
nuclear graphite have largely utilised optical microscopy \citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a,
Taylor2016}.  Optical microscopy generates datasets based on thousands of pores,
but operates at a low resolution and magnification relative to scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) analysis \citep{Huang2014,
huang2021statistical,ARREGUIMENA2022112047}.
 Additionally, the low magnification
required to image large areas via optical microscopy cannot identify pores below a
pore area in some cases as large as 50 µm², thus excluding a significant
proportion of the pore size distribution indicated by the experimental methods
mentioned above \citep{Taylor2016}. 

SEM characterises very much smaller pores.  Once the fundamental instrumental
and sample preparation challenges have been overcome, its main drawback is that
the area of each micrograph is very small compared to the surface of one face of the  representative elementary volume (REV). (The REV is that volume of sample which, when replicated up to the sample volume, gives an agglomeration of replicates which has the same macroscopic properties as the sample itself.) 
It also has has the obvious drawback that only the
surface of the sample is observed, not its interior network of voids. In this work,  we overcome both of these limitations - the first by stitching together
multiple micrographs, and the second by inverse modelling, as described below. 

Samples for both optical and electron microscopy are often resin impregnated,
with a dye to contrast the resin against the sample bulk.  However, resin
impregnation makes it more difficult to distinguish surface irregularities from
depth-penetrating pores, and precludes the application of a series of
experimental techniques to the same sample. So no impregnation was carried out
in this work.



\subsection{Principles of this study}

Porosity differs between grades of nuclear graphite due to varying constituents
and manufacturing processes. In this work we consider only surface-accessible or
`open' porosity, and exclude any closed pore volume which is inaccessible from
the surface, such as that found within or between the onion-skin-like layers of
`gilsos' within the gilsocarbon in UK AGRs. We define `channel porosity' as the
total area of voids observed at the surface of a porous sample within the area
of a particular micrograph as a percentage of the total surface area of the
micrograph. This would equate  to the total accessible porosity of the sample if
every surface void was the entrance to a channel, approximately normal to the
observed surface, of constant cross-sectional area, extending from the observed
side of the sample to the opposite side - hence our designation `channel'
porosity.  It is evidently not the same as open porosity, which includes hidden
connections between the channels, and pores or variations in channel size that
cannot be seen from the surface.    
Nevertheless channel and open porosity are often implicitly equated during image
analysis \citep{Kane2011a,Huang2019,huang2021statistical}.

As evident from Section \ref{subsec:Experimental techniques}, there is no
individual experimental technique that is capable of characterising the full
pore size distribution observable in nuclear grade graphite.  So we adopt a
multi-technique approach.  The experimental inputs are combined using the new version 3 of the 
PoreXpert inverse modelling package.  We use mercury intrusion porosimetry
characteristics and GCMC interpreted gas adsorption that we have published
previously \citep{Jones2018}. That previous work used porosimetry, surface
adsorption and pycnometry as input.  In this work, we calculate channel porosity
via computational analysis of composite micrographs, instead of measuring open
porosity by pycnometry. 

Inverse modelling never produces a single answer, instead, in this case, it
generates a range of possible void structures all of which would have
experimental characteristics closely matching the input data. Therefore we
choose the most statistically representative structure amongst many stochastic
generations.  We rigorously validate the method by comparing the open porosities
calculated from the channel porosity by the inverse modelling packages with the open
porosities measured by the manufacturers of the samples.

\subsection{Sample preparation}
Cuboids were subsampled from into cuboids of side lengths 7 mm, providing 3
cuboids per grade. Samples were polished with SiC polishing pads up to a grit
size of P5000, to minimise topographical variations induced by sample
preparation that may cause artefacts during SEM imaging or low pressure gas
adsorption \citep{Fang2022,Jones2018}. Samples were sonicated in 2-propanol for
24 h to remove silica particles introduced during polishing, then dried under
vacuum for 12 h at $305 \pm 5 \,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ using the BELPREP-vac
(MicrotracBEL, Japan) to remove residual 2-propanol.


\subsection{Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)}

Micrographs were acquired using a JEOL JSM-IT510 Scanning Electron Microscope
operating at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV in Secondary Electron mode. To
minimise the REV problem described above, a contiguous set of 225 micrographs
was captured for each sample using the Image Montage function within the JEOL
InScope software, operating a motorised stage, with the settings and output
parameter in Table ~\ref{tab:microscopy_parameters}. During acquisition, the
stigmation,\todo{stigmatization ?} contrast, and brightness were automatically adjusted. A subsequent
metadata analysis is detailed in the Supplementary Information Table 2, and

\subsection{Composite assembly}

The individual micrographs were assembled into a single composite image, via an
enhanced phase correlation method, operated as a plugin of the Fiji software
package \citep{schindelin2012fiji, Preibisch2009}. The approach optimises
micrograph positions globally to sub-pixel accuracy and smooths the transitions
between micrographs by a non-linear intensity blending function.  The resulting
composite images exhibit no visible delineation between individual micrographs.
This produces a total area comparable with previous optical microscopy studies
of nuclear graphite, but with the crucial difference that on zooming in, fine
detail can be observed down to 100 nanometres \ref{tab:microscopy_parameters}.
Hence channel porosity can be clearly resolved over a wide area. All composite
images were cropped to a size of 1,350 µm $\times$ 950 µm. 

\subsection{Computation of Channel Porosity}

\subsubsection{Intensity/Greyscale Thresholding}

For an 8-bit micrograph with pixel values \(0 \le E(x,y) \le 255\), the
intensity histogram \(H(i)\) for \(i=0,1,\dots,255\) is \begin{equation}\tag{i}
H(i) = \sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{y=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\{\,E(x,y)=i\,\}, \end{equation}
where \(M\) and \(N\) are the image width and height (in pixels), and
\begin{equation}\tag{ii} \mathbf{1}\{\,E(x,y)=i\,\} = \begin{cases} 1, &
\text{if } E(x,y)=i,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \end{equation} The
double sum ranges over all pixels, producing a 256-bin histogram \(H(i)\), with
each bin equal to the number of pixels whose greyscale value is \(i\).

\caption{(a) Composite SEM micrograph of IG-430 Sample C at 1000 ×
magnification, with a high-resolution inset highlighting the resolution with
which microstructural features are being captured. (b) 8-bit intensity histogram
for IG-430 Sample F.}

 A global intensity threshold was applied to each
composite micrograph to generate a binary image, segmenting porosity and bulk.
Automatic global thresholding algorithms available in Fiji were attempted but
found to be unsuitable for this dataset (see Supplementary Information). As with
previous works, an appropriate threshold was determined manually from the
resultant intensity histograms by visual inspection of the micrographs
\citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016,huang2021statistical}.

Threshold selection was guided by two physical criteria. Firstly, for
consistency with the mercury porosimetry data by capturing the pore throats
rather than the contours of the pore bodies. Secondly, the exclusion of closed
pore volume incidentally exposed by sample preparation, which appear as brighter
grey regions. Both criteria were met by the application of a threshold which
captures the darkest regions in SEM micrographs, excluding closed pore volume
and capturing pore throats. Nominally, this translated to an intensity threshold
of $0.95 \pm 0.075$, normalized between 0-1 over an 8-bit range.

	\subsubsection{Pore Diameter Thresholding}
  Following binarisation, a minimum pore size threshold was applied to
  distinguish genuine porosity from surface rugosity and imaging artefacts, as
  in previous optical microscopy based studies of nuclear graphite
  \citep{KANE2018369,Taylor2016} Threshold selection was informed by a
  sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Information).

  Based on the sensitivity analysis and inspection of the resulting
  segmentations (Figure \ref{fig:improvedporediademo})  a pore area threshold of
  2 µm² was determined to be the optimal compromise.

  This threshold minimised Type I errors (the incorrect classification of other
  features, as porosity) while minimising Type II errors (the erroneous
  exclusion of genuine pores). This threshold was applied across all samples.

Coloured circles highlight pores that are included at one threshold but are excluded at the next highest value. Thresholds applied (a–f, left-to-right, top-to-bottom): None, 0.5 µm\(^2\), 1 µm\(^2\), 2 µm\(^2\), 4 µm\(^2\), and 8 µm\(^2\).}

\subsubsection{Channel Porosity Analysis and Calculation}
Following the application of the intensity and diameter thresholds, channel
porosity was quantified using the Analyse Particles function in Fiji. This
identifies all pixels surpassing the intensity threshold, generating connected
regions, pores, via an 8-connected (Moore neighbourhood) flood-fill algorithm.
Any two pixels that exceed the intensity threshold and are either directly
adjacent or diagonally connected are classified as belonging to the same region
(pore). The area and diameter per pore is calculated by converting the pixel
count into physical area. 

The result of this full workflow on a representative micrograph is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:C1-ig430f fused cropped 8 bit one color threshed subsample 2um
areathresh}. This analysis yields both a channel porosity (\%) and a channel
porosity size distribution which are presented in the Results section.

\subsection{Experimentally-Derived Dataset}

In conjunction with the SEM-based inputs to this multi-technique
characterisation, this study utilised mercury intrusion porosimetry and gas
adsorption isotherms, previously measured by Jones et al. \citep{Jones2018}, who
also provide full experimental details.

The samples analysed in that work were taken from the same parent blocks of
IG-110 and IG-430 as those prepared for the SEM analysis in this study to ensure
direct comparability. For the purposes of the modelling performed here, the key
parameters from the that work were adopted; for porosimetry, advancing and
receding contact angles of 140$^{\circ}$ and 130$^{\circ}$ and a
surface tension of 0.480 N m$^{-1}$, and graphite density of
2.26 g cm$^{-3}$. 

\section{Modelling}

The modelling software PoreXpert proceeds inversely from effect, the
multi-technique percolation curve, to cause, a 3-dimensional void network. The
package constructs an 8-dimensional parameter space, composed of 5 numerical
parameters representing the physical characteristics of the pore network, and 3
constraining Boolean parameters. A Boltzmann-annealed amoeboid simplex searches
over this parameter space to find the set of parameters that produces a
percolation curve minimally different from the intrusion curve derived from
mercury porosimetry and gas adsorption.  The intrusion curve is extended to
smaller void sizes than Hg porosimetry could non-destructively probe, by GCMC
interpretation of gas adsorption (Figure \ref{fig:percolationcurve}) The
total intrusion data spans a total pore diameter range from 0.4 nm to 115 µm.
The intrusion curve is formed of experimental output from both methods, as
mercury porosimetry cannot accurately probe the smallest voids of interest due
to damage to the internal microstructure \cite{Jones2020}. The model output is a
simulated pore network with porosity and percolation properties matching the
experimental measurements (Figure \ref{fig:PX3dnetwork}).

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{./Media/Fit to percolation curve.png}
  \caption{Example of a percolation curve generated by the PoreXpert software(WILL IMPROVE CAPTION)}
  \label{fig:percolationcurve}
\end{figure}

During modelling, the approximation type reflecting the depth of probing into
the porous structure is selected by the user (Figure \ref{fig:pxapproxtypes}).
Approximation Type 2 \textit{Surface connected throats and the visible volumes
of connected pores} was determined to be the optimal approximation for the
channel porosity dataset following examination of the micrographs indicating
that significant portions of the underlying pore bodies were captured during
segmentation.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{./Media/bradapproximationtypesv2.png}
  \caption{Representation of approximation Types 1–4. White regions indicate
   porosity visible to the SEM, while black represents bulk. (a)
   Type 1. Surface-connected throats only. (b) Type 2. Surface-connected throats along with the
   visible volumes of connected pores. (c) Type 3. Surface-connected throats, visible
   volumes of connected pores, and any throats located beneath them. (d) Type 4. All
   vertically aligned features in the top layer, including both
   surface-connected and unconnected elements.}
  \label{fig:pxapproxtypes}
\end{figure}

  Channel porosity (\%) per sample, as well as the minimum, and maximum pore
  diameters, 1.128 µm and 15 µm respectively, were input into the modelling
  software. The lower bound represents the minimum pore diameter threshold
  applied, while the upper pore diameter threshold represents the maximum pore
  diameter successfully captured by this method (Figure \ref{fig:IG110LogPSDs},
  \ref{fig:IG430LogPSDs}). These pore diameter bounds enable the  model to adjust
  the extent of the pore size distribution which the channel porosity provides the input for, with
  porosity smaller and larger these bounds modelled via the experimentally
  derived data.

  Stochastic generations for multiple structure types were performed to identify
  the most representative structure type. For each sample, 10 stochastic
  generations were modelled, and the most representative sample identified using
  via sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Information). The chosen stochastic
  generation per sample was used to generate the final model outputs.

\section{Discussion}

Modelling outputs were highly congruent with experimentally derived open
porosity figures provided by the manufacturer for both IG-110 and IG-430,
providing confidence in the full scale multi-technique characterisation
approach here developed (Table ~\ref{tab:materialstable}, Table
~\ref{tab:Openporositytable}).

Huang \textit{et al.} ~\citep{huang2021statistical} found that channel porosity
as classified by SEM was 3\% for IG-110, which is in close agreement with the
range derived in this work. Interpolation via the adjustment of the intensity
threshold to match previous optical microscopy works (i.e., pore contours are
classified, as opposed to pore throats) produces results consistent with those
works. Kane \textit{et al.} derive a channel porosity of 14.73\% for IG-110,
with Huang and Tang deriving 14\% for the same grade, congruent with the 13-14\%
channel porosity derived by this work following interpolation \citep{Kane2011a,
Huang2019}.


Nevertheless, artefacts exist in the segmentation process which limit the
representativeness of the pore size distribution.

The use of the Moore neighbourhood connectivity criterion, defining two or
more pores connected by even a single adjacent pixel as one pore, agglomerates
some proportion of the porosity. This would result in a rightward shift in the
pore size distribution relative to the distribution which would be derived
from an unagglomerated dataset. The use of a simple connectivity algorithm
means that a single pore with an intersecting feature beneath the intensity
threshold is fragmented into multiple pores, as some of these fragments fall
below the pore diameter threshold and are discarded. This leads to an leftward
shift of the pore size distribution. The extent to which pore fragmentation
cancels out the effect of pore agglomeration was not here determined. 

In future works, the use of a more sophisticated connectivity algorithm such
as the watershed algorithm, may be able to reduce of agglomeration and
fragmentation artefacts. A more complete solution would be to resin-impregnate
the samples, as this would remove the need for the intensity thresholding
stage altogether, although this was not here attempted as the intention of
this work was to demonstrate the effectiveness of the multi-technique
characterisation approach as it would be applied to heterogeneous samples,
where the same sample must be characterised by all techniques, which resin
impregnation would have precluded.

  \section{Conclusion}

This work has met the original objective in successfully demonstrating a
multi-technique characterisation approach the full pore size
distribution of nuclear grade graphite, from 0.4 nm to 115 µm. This approach
successfully generated a percolation curve that yields an open porosity value in
close agreement with the value determined experimentally by He pycnometry,  with
the modelled open porosity values for both IG-110 and IG-430 being within 0.56\%
and 0.71\% of the experimentally derived values respectively.
