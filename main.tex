\begin{abstract}

Nuclear grade graphite is a core component of Generation IV graphite-moderated
reactors. Reliable characterisation of the microporous network is crucial for
safe and optimal performance. The microstructure, in particular porosity,
dictates material properties and the evolution of those properties under
operational conditions. A novel multi-technique characterisation approach was
developed, inversely modelling from Scanning Electron Microscopy, gas
adsorption, and mercury porosimetry data, representing pores from 0.4 nm to 115
µm in diameter. This novel approach has successfully generated pore networks
with characteristics in close agreement with values determined experimentally by
helium pycnometry, with the modelled open porosity values for IG-110 and IG-430
within 0.56\% and 0.71\% of the experimentally derived values respectively.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of this full scale characterisation,
and provide confidence in the ability of this approach to generate
representative pore networks for further grades of both virgin and irradiated
nuclear graphites.

\section{Introduction}
Graphite is employed as a moderator, neutron reflector and structural component
in several Generation IV reactor designs, including the High Temperature Gas
Reactor (HTGR) and Molten Salt Reactor (MSR) \citep{international2025iaea,
international2023iaeaMSRs, MARSDENgeniv}. Nuclear grade graphite, engineered
for this purpose, is characterised by exceptionally low boron content, high
structural strength, thermal stability, and a high scattering and low absorbent
neutron cross-section \citep{Marsden2016}. 

The internal microstructure, primarily porosity, of nuclear grade graphite plays
a key role in its mechanical and thermo-physical properties and the evolution of
these properties under irradiation \citep{MARSDENgeniv}. Porosity differs
between grades of nuclear graphite due to varying manufacturing processes and
inputs. Channel porosity, a subset of total open porosity, is here defined as
the observable void fraction on the sample surface, essentially the surface
accessible porosity.  For the purposes of this work, the microstructure of
nuclear grade graphite is separated into open porosity, as measured by
experimental methods, and channel porosity, as estimated by SEM analysis.

Key performance and safety characteristics relevant to HTGR and MSR operation,
such as oxidation, gas release and thermal degradation, and their evolution
under irradiation are modulated by both porosity types.
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047}. 

For HTGRs, oxidation rates are relevant in fault conditions involving air
ingress, and are typically higher where open porosity is distributed uniformly
\citep{PAUL2022132,GraphitePIRT}. Additionally, porosity strongly affects
diffusion, in turn influencing degradation rate, determining whether the
behaviour under oxidative conditions will be diffusion-controlled or
kinetic-controlled \citep{KANE2018369, MATTHEWS2021111245, PAUL2022132}. In
MSRs, exclusion of salt infiltration into the graphite bulk requires a channel
pore diameter of 1 µm \citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt}. Furthermore, impedance of
diffusion of gaseous fission products into the graphite bulk requires a He
permeability of 1 %\times 10\superscript{-8} cm²s\superscript{-1} cm,
corresponding to a 100 nm channel pore diameter \citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt,
SONG201436}. 

Among the primary experimental techniques for microstructural characterisation
are mercury (Hg) intrusion porosimetry, helium (He) pycnometry, gas adsorption,
and X-ray-Computed Tomography (XCT) \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047,
JONES2020256HgHe, CONTESCU2019663, Jones2020,  KANE2020291,Liu2017,
MOSTAFAVI2013410, BARHLI2017357,ZHANGZXF-5Q-infiltration2016, LAUDONE201461,
LEE201438}. No individual experimental technique is capable of characterising
the full pore size distribution observable in nuclear grade graphite
\citep{Jones2018}.

XCT is of interest to the multiscale characterisation here developed, given the
capability of resolving both open and closed porosity, as well as its
non-destructive nature. Several works have employed XCT to characterise the
microstructure of nuclear grade graphite and derive insights
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047,Liu2017, MOSTAFAVI2013410, KANE2020291,
BARHLI2017357}. Recent XCT characterisation efforts relevant to MSR and HTGR
operation include real-time tracking of salt infiltration and modelling of the
relation between microstructure and oxidation rate \citep{ZHANG2024102016,
PAUL2022132}. However, XCT has limited spatial resolution, typically around 1
µm³ for nuclear grade graphites and is further limited by the application of a
minimum pore volume threshold, typically on the order of 10 µm³
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047}.  XCT has therefore not formed part of the
multi-technique characterisation here developed.

Previous studies have also involved quantifying channel porosity by optical or
electron microscopy \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047, Huang2014, Huang2019,
Kane2011a, Taylor2016, huang2021statistical}. Most of these works have employed
optical microscopy (OM), generating datasets based on thousands of pores, but
operating at a low resolution and magnification relative to Scanning Electron
Microscopy (SEM) analysis \citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016}. In addition,
the low magnification required to image large areas via OM necessitates a
minimum pore size cutoff in some cases as high as 50 µm², excluding a
significant proportion of the pore size distribution indicated by experimental
methods \citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016}.  Operating at a higher
resolution and magnification than previous OM studies, this work employed SEM to
quantify channel porosity, forming a constraining parameter to multi-technique
inverse modelling of nuclear grade graphite microstructure.

For the first time, SEM data has been integrated with experimental void
distributions obtained from mercury porosimetry and Grand Canonical Monte Carlo
(GCMC) modelled gas adsorption data. The modelling here developed therefore
covers the full pore size of nuclear grade graphite, from nanometre to
micrometre scale. This characterisation is also more complete than previous
works, with SEM characterising those pores too large to be characterised by gas
adsorption and too small for mercury porosimetry \citep{Jones2018}. 

The validation of this integrated approach is contingent upon its ability to
generate a full percolation curve—derived from the combination of SEM-derived
channel porosity, mercury porosimetry data, and GCMC modelled gas
adsorption—that yields an open porosity value in close agreement with the value
determined experimentally by Helium (He) pycnometry.

\subsection{Sample preparation}
Cuboids were subsampled from virgin graphite blocks, with dimensions of
approximately 10 mm $\times$ 10 mm $\times$ 100 mm. The subsamples were further subsampled
into cuboids of side lengths 7 mm, providing 3 cuboids per grade. Samples were
polished via SiC polishing pads up to a grit size of P5000, to minimise
topographical variations induced by sample preparation that may cause artefacts
during SEM imaging or low pressure gas adsorption \citep{Fang2022,Jones2018}.
Samples were sonicated in 2-propanol for 24 h to remove silica particles
introduced during polishing, then dried under vacuum for 12 h at $305 \pm 5
\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ using the BELPREP-vac (MicrotracBEL, Japan) to remove
residual 2-propanol.

The multi-technique characterisation here developed requires the same sample to
undergo SEM, He pycnometry, Hg porosimetry and gas adsorption. Resin
impregnation was therefore omitted as it renders inaccessible the pore volumes
which the experimental techniques probe, precluding the application of the full
series of methods to a single sample.

\subsection{Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)}

Micrographs were acquired using a JEOL JSM-IT510 Scanning Electron Microscope
operating at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV in Secondary Electron mode. For
each sample a contiguous set of 225 micrographs was captured using the Image
Montage function within the JEOL InScope software, operating a motorised stage,
applied parameters are tabulated (Table ~\ref{tab:microscopy_parameters}).
During acquisition, stigmation, contrast, and brightness were automatically
adjusted. A subsequent metadata analysis is detailed in the Supplementary
Information (Table ~\ref{tab:contrast-brightness-summary}), confirmed that
variations in contrast and brightness were negligible.

\subsection{Composite assembly}

The individual micrographs were assembled into a single composite, via an
enhanced phase correlation method, operated as a plugin of the Fiji software
package \citep{schindelin2012fiji, Preibisch2009}. The approach optimises
micrograph position globally to sub-pixel accuracy and smooths the transitions
between micrographs by a non-linear intensity blending function.  The resulting
composites exhibit no visible delineation between individual micrographs. This
produces a total area comparable with previous OM studies of nuclear graphite,
and resolves channel porosity clearly (Figure \ref{fig:histogramandcomposite}
\citep{Huang2019,Kane2011a,huang2021statistical}).

\subsection{Computation of Channel Porosity}


\subsubsection{Intensity/Greyscale Thresholding}

For the construction of the histogram of intensity, where porosity is to be 
distinguished, per 8-bit micrograph with pixel values \(0 \le E(x,y) \le 255\) 
the intensity histogram \(H(i)\) is computed for \(i=0,1,\dots,255\) (Figure 
~\ref{fig:histogramandcomposite}).
\begin{equation}\tag{i}
H(i) = \sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{y=1}^{N} I\{E(x,y)=i\},
\end{equation}
where \(M\) and \(N\) are the micrograph width and height (in pixels), and
\begin{equation}\tag{ii}
I\{E(x,y)\} =
\begin{cases}
I, & \text{if the pixel at }(x,y)\text{ has intensity }i,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The indicator function \(I\{E(x,y)\}\) increments the bin
corresponding to that greyscale value. The double sum
\(\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{y=1}^N\) performs  across each pixel in the micrograph
resulting in a histogram \(H(i)\) of 256 bins, each bin representing number of
pixels in the micrograph at each greyscale value \(i\).

A global intensity threshold was applied to each composite micrograph to
generate a binary image, segmenting porosity and bulk.  Automatic global
thresholding algorithms available in Fiji were attempted but found to be
unsuitable for this dataset (see Supplementary Information).s with previous works,
threshold was determined manually from the resultant intensity histograms
\citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016,,huang2021statistical}.

Threshold selection was guided by two physical criteria. Firstly, for consistency
with the mercury porosimetry data by capturing the pore throats rather than the
contours of the pore bodies. Secondly, the exclusion of Closed Pore Volume (CPV)
incidentally exposed by sample preparation, which appear as brighter grey
regions. Both criteria were met by the application of a threshold which captures
the darkest regions in SEM micrographs, excluding CPV and capturing pore
throats. Nominally, this translated to an intensity threshold of $0.95 \pm 0.075$,
normalized between 0-1 over an 8-bit range.

	\subsubsection{Pore Diameter Thresholding}
  Following binarisation, a minimum pore size threshold was applied to
  distinguish genuine porosity from surface rugosity and imaging artefacts, as
  in previous OM based studies of nuclear graphite
  \citep{KANE2018369,Taylor2016} Threshold selection was informed by a
  sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Information).

  Based on the sensitivity analysis and inspection of the resulting
  segmentations (Figure \ref{fig:improvedporediademo})  a pore area threshold of
  2 µm² was determined to be the optimal compromise.

  This threshold minimised Type I errors (the incorrect classification of other
  features, as porosity) while minimising Type II errors (the erroneous
  exclusion of genuine pores). This threshold was applied across all samples.

\subsubsection{Channel Porosity Analysis and Calculation}
Following the application of the intensity and diameter thresholds, channel
porosity was quantified using the Analyse Particles function in Fiji. This
identifies all pixels surpassing the intensity threshold, generating connected
regions, pores, via an 8-connected (Moore neighbourhood) flood-fill algorithm.
Any two pixels that exceed the intensity threshold and are either directly
adjacent or diagonally connected are classified as belonging to the same region
(pore). The area and diameter per pore is calculated by converting the pixel
count into physical area. 

The result of this full workflow on a representative micrograph is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:C1-ig430f fused cropped 8 bit one color threshed subsample 2um
areathresh}. This analysis yields both a channel porosity (\%) and a channel
porosity size distribution which are presented in the Results section.

\subsection{Experimentally-Derived Dataset}

In conjunction with the SEM-based inputs to this multi-technique
characterisation, this study utilised existing experimental data for helium
pycnometry, mercury intrusion porosimetry, and gas adsorption. The dataset was
extracted from the comprehensive characterisation study of Jones \textit{et al} and
replicated for this study \citep{Jones2018}.

The samples analysed in that work were taken from the same parent blocks of
IG-110 and IG-430 as those prepared for the SEM analysis in this study to ensure
direct comparability. For the purposes of the modelling performed here, the key
parameters from the that work were adopted. For porosimetry, advancing and
receding contact angles of 140$^{\circ}$ and 130$^{\circ}$ and for pycnometry a
surface tension of 0.480 N m$^{-1}$ as well as a theoretical graphite density of
2.26 g cm$^{-3}$. Full experimental details for these techniques can be found in
the original publication \citep{Jones2018}.

\section{Modelling}

The inverse modelling software PoreXpert proceeds inversely from effect
(the percolation) to cause (the void network). The package constructs an
8-dimensional parameter space, composed of 5 numerical parameters which
represent the physical characteristics of the pore network, and 3 constraining
Boolean parameters. A Boltzmann-annealed amoeboid simplex searches over this
parameter space to find the set of parameters that produces a percolation curve
minimally different from the intrusion curve derived from Hg porosimetry and
N$_2$/Kr adsorption. The intrusion curve is formed of experimental output from
both methods as mercury porosimetery cannot accurately probe the smallest voids
of interest due to damage of the internal microstructure. The percolation
characteristic is therefore extended to smaller void sizes by GCMC
interpretation derived from adsorption data spanning a total pore diameter range

  Channel porosity (\%) per sample, as well as the minimum, and maximum pore
  diameters, 1.128 µm and 15 µm respectively, were input into the modelling
  software. The lower bound represents the minimum pore diameter threshold
  applied, while the upper pore diameter threshold represents the maximum pore
  diameter successfully captured by this method (Figure \ref{fig:IG110LogPSDs},
  \ref{fig:IG430LogPSDs}). These pore diameter bounds enable the  model to adjust
  the extent of the PSD which the channel porosity provides the input for, with
  porosity smaller and larger these bounds modelled via the experimentally
  derived data.

  Stochastic generations for multiple structure types were performed to identify
  the most representative structure type. For each sample, 10 stochastic
  generations were modelled, and the most representative sample identified using
  via sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Information). The chosen stochastic
  generation per sample was used to generate the final model outputs.

\section{Discussion}

Huang \textit{et al.} ~\citep{huang2021statistical} found that channel porosity
as classified by SEM was 3\% for IG-110, which is in close agreement with the
range derived in this work. In order to compare with previous works, a
comparative analysis was performed by reduction of the intensity threshold to
segment the full pore contours, consistent with the segmentation of those works.
This produced channel porosity values between 13\% and 14\% for both grades,
which is consistent with the 14.73\% for IG-110 derived by Kane \textit{et al.},
and the 14\% figure determined by Huang and Tang \citep{Kane2011a, Huang2019}.

Nevertheless, artefacts exist in the segmentation process which limit the
representativeness of the pore size distribution.

  The use of the Moore neighbourhood connectivity criterion, defining two or
  more pores connected by even a single adjacent pixel as one pore, agglomerates
  some proportion of the porosity. Notably, this would not alter the channel
  porosity, nor the modelling for which it is the key input. However, this would
  result in a rightward shift in the pore size distribution relative to the
  distribution which would be derived from an unagglomerated dataset.

  In addition, the use of a simple connectivity algorithm means that a single
  pore with an intersecting feature beneath the intensity threshold, is
  fragmented into multiple pores. Provided that one or more of these fragments
  falls below the pore diameter threshold, they are erroneously discarded from
  the dataset. This leads to an underestimation of the channel porosity and a
  leftward shift of the pore size distribution. The extent to which pore
  fragmentation cancels out the effect of pore agglomeration was not here determined.

\subsection{Pore Diameter Thresholding Sensitivity Analysis}

A sensitivity analysis, in conjunction with visual analysis as per (Figure
~\ref{fig:improvedporediademo}) was conducted to establish a justifiable lower
limit for pore segmentation. Based on the data in Table
\ref{tab:thresholdsandvariationsIG430F} and visual inspection of the resulting
segmentation exemplified in (Figure \ref{fig:improvedporediademo}), an area
threshold of 2 µm² was selected. This value was determined to be the optimal
compromise for excluding surface rugosity and retaining genuine porosity.

\subsection{Attempted Automatic Global Intensity Thresholding}

None of the 17 automatic global intensity thresholding algorithms available in
ImageJ were able to distinguish between porosity and bulk in this dataset,
(Figure ~\ref{fig:Try All Thresholding Methods}). For each sample, the histogram
\(H(i)\) produced by this method exhibited only a single peak, yielding no
statistically distinct intensity classes (Figure \ref{fig:histogramnobimodal}).
This is attributed to the small area fraction represented by porosity at the
operational magnification and resolution. This low fraction fails to yield a
secondary peak for the pore phase in the histogram. These algorithms operate by
identifying a threshold value between peaks and are therefore not functional for
this dataset.
