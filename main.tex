\begin{abstract}
Nuclear grade graphite is a critical component of those Generation IV reactors
which are graphite-moderated. Reliable characterisation of its microporous
network is crucial for safe and optimal performance. The microstructure, in
particular porosity, dictates material properties and the evolution of those
properties under operational conditions (i.e., oxidation rates, gas diffusion,
and thermal degradation). This project develops and initially validates a
methodology for characterising the surface porosity of IG-110 and IG-430 nuclear
graphites via computational analysis of composite Scanning Electron Microscopy
(SEM) micrographs, covering an FOV (Field of View) comparable with previous
Optical Microscopy (OM) -based works (mm\(^2\) scale). Initial integration of
surface porosity data into a new version of the PoreXpert void network and pore
fluid simulation framework, integrated with Hg porosimetry and N$_2$ adsorption,
produced physically plausible preliminary models of the pore network and
simulated pore-fluid flow properties such as tortuosity, diffusivity, and
permeability. This enhanced characterisation method forms a further useful
methodology in refining models of graphite behaviour, ultimately contributing to
the safe and optimal operation of current and proposed graphite-moderated
nuclear reactors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Graphite is employed as a moderator, neutron reflector and structural component
in Generation IV reactors \citep{MARSDENgeniv}. Nuclear grade graphite is
engineered for this purpose, characterised by exceptionally low boron content,
high structural strength, thermal stability, and a high scattering and low
absorbent neutron cross-section \citep{Marsden2016}. 

The internal microstructure of nuclear grade graphite plays a key role in its
mechanical and thermo-physical properties, as well the evolution of these
properties under irradiation \citep{MARSDENgeniv}. Porosity differs between
grades of nuclear graphite due to varying manufacturing processes and inputs,
but performance and safety-related phenomena, such as oxidation, gas release and
thermal degradation, are modulated by it \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047}. For
example, oxidation rates are typically higher where open porosity is distributed
uniformly. \citep{PAUL2022132}. Additionally, porosity strongly affects
diffusion, in turn influencing degradation rate, determining whether the
behaviour under oxidative conditions will be diffusion-controlled or
kinetic-controlled \citep{KANE2018369, MATTHEWS2021111245, KANE2018369}. 

Among the primary experimental techniques for the indirect characterisation of
porosity are mercury (Hg) intrusion porosimetry, helium (He) pycnometry, and
nitrogen (N$_2$) adsorption \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047, JONES2020256HgHe,
CONTESCU2019663}. No individual experimental technique is capable of
characterising the full pore size distribution. 

The limitations  of these techniques increase the attractiveness of direct
characterisation methods, where the pores themselves are characterised. Previous
studies involved quantifying surface visible porosity by optical or electron
microscopy \citep{Huang2014, Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016,
huang2021statistical, ARREGUIMENA2022112047}. Most of these works have employed
optical microscopy (OM), generating datasets based on thousands of pores, but
operating at a low resolution and magnification relative to SEM analysis
\citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016}. In addition, the low magnification
required to image large areas via OM necessitates a minimum pore size cutoff in
some cases as high as 50 µm², excluding a significant proportion of the pore
size distribution indicated by experimental methods \citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a,
Taylor2016}. 

This work develops a method of quantifying the channel porosity of nuclear-grade
graphites via Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) micrographs, as a new input to
the PoreXpert\texttrademark inverse modelling package. Channel porosity is here defined as the
void fraction observable on the sample surface. This is clearly different from
the total open porosity as many pores are hidden from the surface.

By operating at a higher resolution and magnification than previous OM studies,
this SEM-based approach yields a more statistically representative channel
porosity size distribution. When integrated with mercury (Hg) porosimetry, and
Grand Canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) modelled N$_2$ adsorption data, a more
representative pore network model is generated. This allows for more accurate
network characteristic calculations, such as permeability or diffusivity.

The validation of this integrated approach is contingent upon its ability to
generate a full percolation curve—derived from the combination of SEM-derived
channel porosity, mercury (Hg) porosimetry data, and Grand Canonical Monte Carlo
(GCMC) modelled N$_2$ adsorption—that yields an open porosity value in close
agreement with that determined experimentally by Helium (He) pycnometry.

\subsection{Sample preparation}
Cuboids were subsampled from virgin graphite blocks, with dimensions of
approximately 10 mm x 10 mm x 100 mm. The subsamples were further subsampled
into cuboids of side lengths 7 mm, providing 3 cuboids per grade. Samples were
polished via SiC polishing pads up to a grit size of P5000, to minimise
topographical variations induced by sample preparation that may cause artefacts
during SEM imaging or low pressure gas adsorption \citep{Fang2022,Jones2018}.
Samples were sonicated in 2-propanol for 24 h to remove silica particles
introduced during polishing, then dried under vacuum for 12 h at $305 \pm 5
\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ using the BELPREP-vac (MicrotracBEL, Japan) to remove
residual 2-propanol.

The multi-technique characterisation here developed requires the same sample to
undergo SEM, He pycnometry, Hg porosimetry and gas adsorption. Resin
impregnation was intentionally omitted as it renders inaccessible the pore
volumes which the experimental techniques probe, precluding the application of
the full series of methods to a single sample.

\subsection{Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)}

High-resolution micrographs for porosity analysis were acquired using a JEOL
JSM-IT510 Scanning Electron Microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 5
kV in Secondary Electron (SE) mode. For each sample a contiguous set of 225
micrographs was captured using the 'Image Montage' function within the JEOL
InScope software, operating a motorised stage, Applied parameters are tabulated
(Table ~\ref{tab:microscopy_parameters}). During acquisition, stigmation,
contrast, and brightness were automatically adjusted. A subsequent metadata
analysis detailed in the Supplementary Information (Table
~\ref{tab:contrast-brightness-summary}), confirmed that variations in contrast
and brightness were negligible.

\subsection{Composite assembly}

The individual micrographs were assembled into a single composite, via an
enhanced phase correlation method, operated as a plugin of the Fiji software
package \citep{schindelin2012fiji, Preibisch2009}. The approach optimises
micrograph position globally to sub-pixel accuracy and smooths the transitions
between micrographs by a non-linear intensity blending function. 

The resulting composites exhibit no visible delineation between individual
micrographs, cover an total area comparable with previous OM studies of nuclear
graphite, and resolve porosity clearly (Figure \ref{fig:IG430C split scaled}
\citep{Huang2019,Kane2011a,huang2021statistical}).

\[
H(i) = \sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{y=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\{E(x,y)=i\},
\]
where \(M\) and \(N\) are the micrograph width and height (in pixels), and
\[
\mathbf{1}\{E(x,y)=i\} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if the pixel at }(x,y)\text{ has intensity }i,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

The indicator function \(\mathbf{1}\{E(x,y)=i\}\) increments the bin
corresponding to that greyscale value. The double sum
\(\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{y=1}^N\) performs  across each pixel in the micrograph
resulting in a histogram \(H(i)\) of 256 bins, each bin representing number of
pixels in the micrograph at each greyscale value \(i\).

A global intensity threshold was applied to each composite micrograph to
generate a binary image, segmenting porosity and bulk. As with previous works,
threshold was determined manually from the resultant intensity histograms
\citep{Taylor2016,Huang2019,huang2021statistical,Kane2011a}. Automatic global
thresholding algorithms available in Fiji were attempted but found to be
unsuitable for this dataset (see Supplementary Information).

Threshold selection was guided by two physical criteria. First, consistency with
the mercury (Hg) porosimetry data, selecting a threshold which captures the pore
throats which appear as the darkest regions in SEM micrographs, rather than the
contours of the pore bodies. Second, the exclusion of Closed Pore Volume (CPV)
incidentally exposed by sample preparation, which appear as brighter grey
regions. Following these criteria, an intensity threshold of $0.95 \pm 0.075$,
normalized between 0-1 over an 8-bit range, was selected and applied across
all samples.

	\subsubsection{Pore Diameter Thresholding}
    
  Following binarisation, a minimum pore size threshold was applied to
  distinguish genuine porosity from surface rugosity and imaging artefacts, as
  previous OM based studies of nuclear graphite \citep{Taylor2016,KANE2018369}
  Threshold selection was informed by a sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary
  Information).

  Based on the sensitivity analysis and inspection of the resulting
  segmentations (Figure \ref{fig:improvedporediademo})  a pore area threshold of
  2 µm\(^2\) (diameter = 1.12 µm) was determined to be the optimal compromise.

  This threshold minimised Type I errors (the incorrect classification of other
  features, as porosity) while minimising Type II errors (the erroneous
  exclusion of genuine pores). This threshold was applied across all samples.

\subsubsection{Channel Porosity Analysis and Calculation}
Following the application of the intensity and diameter thresholds, channel
porosity was quantified using the “Analyse Particles” function in Fiji. This
function identifies all pixels surpassing the intensity threshold, generating
connected regions, pores, via an 8-connected (Moore neighbourhood) flood-fill
algorithm. Any two pixels that exceed the intensity threshold and are either
directly adjacent or diagonally connected are classified as belonging to the
same region (pore). The area and diameter per pore is calculated by converting
the pixel count into physical area. 

The result of this full workflow on a representative micrograph is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:C1-ig430f fused cropped 8 bit one color threshed subsample 2um
areathresh}. This analysis yields both a channel porosity (\%) and a channel
porosity size distribution.

\subsection{Helium (He) Pycnometry}

Helium pycnometry measures the volume of solids via gas displacement, applying
Boyle's Law for multiple states  from which density can be derived for a known
mass. 


The dataset containing Skeletal density measurements, and calculations of Closed
Pore Volume (CPV), Open Pore Volume (OPV), and Specific Pore Volume (SPV)
generated by Jones et al. was used \citep{Jones2018}. This dataset represents
helium pycnometry measurements performed on IG-110 and IG-430 sampled from the same block as those
samples which underwent SEM imaging in this work.

A Pycnomatic ATC pycnometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Italy) was used to
generate this dataset. Measurements were performed at a temperature of 20.00 ±
0.01\textdegree{}C, for ten replicates per sample, calculating the arithmetic
mean. 

Solid phase volume $V_{\mathrm{SOLID}}$ was calculated assuming a theoretical
density of 2.26 g cm$^{-3}$ for an idealised graphite crystal. 

\subsection{Mercury (Hg) Intrusion Porosimetry}
Mercury intrusion porosimetry operates on the principle that the pressure at
which a non-wetting fluid intrudes a pore is inversely proportional to the
diameter of that pore.

The exact physical relationship between diameter and applied pressure is
governed by the Laplace Equation (Eq. ~\ref{eq:washburn})
	
	\begin{equation} \label{eq:washburn}
		d = \frac{-4\gamma \cos \theta}{P}
	\end{equation}

where
$d$ (m) is Pore diameter
$\gamma$ (N/m) is Surface tension of the fluid
$\theta$ (degrees) is Contact angle of the fluid with the surface
$P$ (Pa) is Pressure

140$^{\circ}$ and 130$^{\circ}$ were the values assumed for advancing and
receding contact angles respectively. 0.480 N m$^{-1}$ was taken for the surface
tension of mercury \citep{VANBRAKEL19811}.  

In this work, the dataset generated by Jones et al. \citep{Jones2018} was used,
representing mercury intrusion porosimetry performed on IG-110 and IG-430
sampled from the same block as those samples which underwent SEM imaging in this work.

\subsection{Nitrogen (N$_2$) Adsorption}
Low-pressure gas adsorption isotherms were obtained using a BELSORP-max (MicrotracBEL, Japan)
volumetric gas adsorption instrument.

The dataset generated by Jones \textit{et al}. \citep{Jones2018} was used in
this work, representing nitrogen adsorption measurements performed on IG-110 and
IG-430 sampled from the same block as those samples which underwent SEM imaging
in this work.

\section{Modelling}

PoreXpert is a quasi-Bayesian modelling software which proceeds inversely from
effect (the percolation) to cause (the void network). The software constructs an
8-dimensional parameter space, composed of 5 numerical parameters which
represent the physical characteristics of the pore network, and 3 constraining
Boolean parameters. A Boltzmann-annealed amoeboid simplex searches over this
parameter space to find the set of parameters that produces a percolation curve
minimally different from the intrusion curve derived from Hg porosimetry and
N$_2$/Kr adsorption. The intrusion curve is formed of experimental output from
both methods as mercury porosimetery cannot reach sufficient pressure to probe
the smallest voids of interest, and therefore, the percolation characteristic is
extended to smaller void sizes by Grand-Canonical Monte-Carlo interpretation of
N$_2$ adsorption. The output is a simulated pore network with the matching
porosity and percolation properties, on which simulations such as pore-fluid
permeability and tortuosity can be performed (Figure \ref{fig:PX3dnetwork}

During modelling, the approximation type reflecting the depth of probing into
the porous structure is selected by the user (Figure
\ref{fig:bradapproximationtypesv2.png}). Approximation Type 2 \textit{Surface
connected throats and the visible volumes of connected pores} was determined to
be the optimal approximation for the channel porosity dataset, following
examination of the micrographs which indicated that significant portions of the
underlying pore bodies were captured during segmentation.

\section{Discussion}

This work provides valuable insights into the microstructure of IG-110 and
IG-430. However, a critical evaluation reveals several limitations, the
improvement of which will form the basis of future work.

  \subsection{Image Segmentation Artefacts: Fragmentation and Agglomeration}

  In this work, the limitations of any single technique are compensated by the
  others. As such, the restriction of the pore diameter range represented by SEM
  does not introduce type II errors to the modelling, as the additional
  techniques characterise the pore size distribution above and below that derived
  from SEM. Nevertheless, artefacts exist in the segmentation process which
  limit the representativeness of the pore size distribution.

  \subsubsection{Pore Agglomeration}
    The use of the Moore neighbourhood connectivity criterion, defines two or
    more pores connected by even a single adjacent pixel, as a single pore.
    Notably, this would not alter the channel porosity, nor the modelling
    for which it is the key input. However, this would result in a rightward
    shift in the pore size distribution.


\subsubsection{Pore Fragmentation}

  In addition, the use of a simple connectivity algorithm means that a single
  pore with an intersecting feature beneath the intensity threshold, is
  fragmented into multiple pores. Provided that one or more of these fragments
  falls below the pore diameter threshold, they are erroneously discarded from
  the dataset. This leads to an underestimation of the channel porosity \% and a
  leftward shift of the pore size distribution.

  Reliable modelling depends on accurate delineation of the pore diameter
  interval, as well as a channel porosity which accurately represents the porosity
  within that interval. Methods of addressing the shortcomings highlighted here
  are addressed in the Future Work section.

  \subsection{Future Work}

  To address the limitations identified and develop the accuracy of modelling
  inputs, several refinements are proposed.
  
  A refined polishing protocol is required to further reduce surface artefacts,
  thereby enhancing edge detection. This should include jig-based polishing, and
  diamond suspensions \citep{GREBENNIKOVA2025155935}. Resin impregnation may
  precede these steps, but would preclude He pycnometry, gas adsorption, and Hg
  intrusion porosimetry on the same sample. Consequently, multi-technique
  characterisation involving resin impregnation would require parallel
  characterisation of separate samples, only valid where the grade is
  sufficiently homogenous. This approach may be inapplicable to heterogenous
  graphite grades where sample preparation involves resin impregnation. 
  
  The application of the Rosin thresholding algorithm for unimodal histograms is
  expected to improve replicability, although not reliability. While an
  automated unimodal threshold may not yield a more representative segmentation
  than the carefully selected manual threshold, its eliminates operator
  subjectivity in this stage, ensuring replicability. It should be noted that
  thresholding in most previous works, even those involving optical microscopy,
  is made manually, with subjectivity acknowledged \citep{Taylor2016, Huang2019,
  huang2021statistical}.

  Replacing the current Moore neighbourhood connectivity criterion in favour of
  a skeleton segmentation type method as per Contescu \textit{et al}
  \citep{CONTESCU2019663},  is proposed to resolve pore agglomeration,
  improving the reliability of the SEM-derived pore size distribution.

\subsection{Attempted Automatic Global Intensity Thresholding}

None of the 17 automatic global intensity thresholding algorithms available in
ImageJ were able to distinguish between porosity and bulk in this dataset,
(Figure ~\ref{fig:Try All Thresholding Methods}). For each sample, the histogram
\(H(i)\) produced by this method exhibited only a single peak, yielding no
statistically distinct intensity classes (Figure \ref{fig:histogramnobimodal}).
This is attributed to the small area fraction represented by porosity at the
operational magnification and resolution. This low fraction fails to yield a
secondary peak for the pore phase in the histogram. These algorithms operate by
identifying a threshold value between peaks and are therefore not functional for
this dataset.
