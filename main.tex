\begin{abstract}

Nuclear grade graphite acts as a structural component and moderator in the remaining advanced gas-cooled nuclear reactors (AGRs) in the UK, and in several next generation (Generation IV) reactor designs.  Its microstructure, in particular porosity, dictates material properties and their
evolution under operational conditions. We present a novel approach to characterising such microstructure, by inverse modelling of porosity estimated from the image analysis of electron micrographs, coupled with previously measured mercury intrusion porosimetry and surface adsorption converted to void size by grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations. The result is a void network comprising pores over the range 0.4 nm to 115 µm, a much larger range than can be obtained from X-Ray computed tomographic (CT) scanning. The method was validated by comparing the porosities of the void network for nuclear graphite grades IG-110 and IG-430 to those quoted by their manufacturer Toyo Tanso.  The estimates are 
within 0.56\% and 0.71\% of manufacturer pycnometry data respectively.
Once the void network has been generated, a range of further simulations of pore fluid behaviour can be carried out.  We illustrate this by displaying the extent of intrusion of molten salt into IG-110 that would occur at high temperature in a Molten Salt Reactor.  The new method has very wide applicability, as it can be used for any rigid porous material which has been studied by microscopy and porosimetry, but for which pycnometry is unavailable or inappropriate.

\section{Introduction}
Graphite is employed as a moderator, neutron reflector and structural component
in the remaining advanced gas-cooled nuclear reactors (AGRs) in the UK, and in several Generation IV reactor designs, including the High Temperature Gas
Reactor (HTGR) and Molten Salt Reactor (MSR) \citep{international2025iaea,
international2023iaeaMSRs, MARSDENgeniv}. Nuclear grade graphite  is
characterised by exceptionally low boron content, high structural strength,
thermal stability, and a high scattering and low absorbent neutron cross-section
\citep{Marsden2016}. 

The internal microstructure of nuclear grade graphite plays
a key role in its mechanical and thermo-physical properties, and the evolution under irradiation of these properties and thermo-chemical properties such as oxidation, gas release and thermal degradation 
\citep{MARSDENgeniv, ARREGUIMENA2022112047}. 
For HTGRs, oxidation rates are relevant in fault conditions involving air
ingress, and are typically higher where open porosity is distributed uniformly
\citep{PAUL2022132,GraphitePIRT}. Additionally, porosity strongly affects
gaseous diffusion, in turn influencing degradation rate, determining whether the
behaviour under oxidative conditions will be diffusion-controlled or
kinetic-controlled \citep{KANE2018369, MATTHEWS2021111245, PAUL2022132}. 
Impedance of
diffusion of gaseous fission products into the graphite bulk requires a He
permeability of less than $1 \times 10^{-8}~\text{cm}^2\text{s}^{-1}~\text{cm}$,
corresponding to pore diameters of 100 nm or less\citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt,
	SONG201436}.
In
MSRs, unwanted salt intrusion can occur into surface pores greater than 1 µm in diameter \citep{McCoy1970MoltenSalt}. 



\subsection{Experimental techniques}
\label{subsec:Experimental techniques}
The primary experimental techniques for microstructural characterisation are
mercury intrusion porosimetry, helium pycnometry, gas adsorption, X-ray computed tomography (X-ray CT), and microscopy.
Mercury intrusion porosimetry is an established technique for characterising
the pore size distribution of nuclear grade graphite
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047, JONES2020256HgHe, CONTESCU2019663, Jones2020}.
However, the technique destroys the voids structure at the highest applied pressures of mercury necessary to probe voids below 100 nm in size, is destructive, and unable to characterise the very
smallest pores as the pressure required induces damage in the microstructure.
Helium pycnometry by contrast is a non-destructive technique that measures the
total open porosity of a sample, but does not provide information on the pore
size distribution. Gas adsorption techniques, such as nitrogen or
krypton adsorption, are also non-destructive and provide detailed
characterisation of the pore size distribution in the nanometre range.

XCT is a non-destructive technique that has also been used to 
characterise the microstructure of nuclear grade graphite 
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047,KANE2020291,Liu2017, MOSTAFAVI2013410,
BARHLI2017357}. Recent XCT characterisation efforts relevant to MSR and HTGR
operation include real-time tracking of salt infiltration and modelling of the
relation between microstructure and oxidation rate \citep{ZHANG2024102016,
PAUL2022132}. However, XCT has limited spatial resolution, typically around 1
µm³ for nuclear grade graphites and is further limited by the application of a
minimum pore volume threshold, typically on the order of 10 µm³
\citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047}.  

Most microscopy studies of nuclear graphite have been optical  \citep{ARREGUIMENA2022112047, Huang2014, Huang2019,
Kane2011a, Taylor2016, huang2021statistical}.

Additionally, the low magnification required to image large areas via OM

SEM characterises very much smaller pores.  Once the fundamental instrumental and sample preparation challenges have been overcome, its main drawback is that the area of each micrograph is very small compared to the representative sample size, i.e. relative to the smallest sample volume which includes all the sample heterogeneity, such that when replicated up to the sample volume the representative gives rise to the macroscopic properties of the sample. It also has has the obvious drawback that only the surface of the sample is observed, not its interior network of voids. We overcome both limitations in this work - the first by stitching together multiple micrographs, and the second by inverse modelling, as described below. 

Samples for both optical and electron microscopy are often resin impregnated, with a dye to contrast the resin against the sample bulk.  However, resin
impregnation makes it more difficult to distinguish surface irregularities from depth-penetrating pores, and precludes the application of a series of experimental techniques to the same sample. So no impregnation was carried out in this work.



\subsection{Principles of this study}

Porosity differs
between grades of nuclear graphite due to varying constituents and manufacturing processes. In this work we consider only surface-accessible or `open' porosity, and exclude any closed pore volume (CPV) which is inaccessible from the surface, such as that found within the onion-skin-like layers of `gilsos' within the gilsocarbon in UK AGRs. 
We define `channel porosity' as the total area of voids observed at the surface of a porous sample within the area of a particular micrograph as a percentage of the total surface area of the micrograph. This would equate  to the total accessible porosity of the sample if every surface void was the entrance to a channel, normal to the observed surface, of constant cross-sectional area, extending from the observed side of the sample to the opposite side - hence our designation `channel' porosity.  It is evidently not the same as the total open porosity, which includes hidden connections between the channels, and pores or variations in channel size that cannot be seen from the surface.

We use mercury intrusion porosimetry characteristics and GCMC interpreted gas adsorption that we have published previously \citep{Jones2018}. That previous work used porosimetry, surface adsorption and pycnometry as input.  In this work, we utilise porosity from imaged-analysed SEMs instead of pycnometry. Inverse modelling produces a range of possible void structures all of which would have experimental characteristics closely matching the input data. Therefore we choose the most statistically representative structure amongst many stochastic generations.  We also rigorously validate the method by comparing the open porosity calculated from the channel porosity by the inverse modeller, with the open porosities measured by the manufacturers of the samples.

\subsection{Sample preparation}
Cuboids were subsampled from virgin graphite blocks, with dimensions of
approximately 10 mm $\times$ 10 mm $\times$ 100 mm. The subsamples were further subsampled
into cuboids of side lengths 7 mm, providing 3 cuboids per grade. Samples were
polished via SiC polishing pads up to a grit size of P5000, to minimise
topographical variations induced by sample preparation that may cause artefacts
during SEM imaging or low pressure gas adsorption \citep{Fang2022,Jones2018}.
Samples were sonicated in 2-propanol for 24 h to remove silica particles
introduced during polishing, then dried under vacuum for 12 h at $305 \pm 5
\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ using the BELPREP-vac (MicrotracBEL, Japan) to remove
residual 2-propanol.


\subsection{Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)}

Micrographs were acquired using a JEOL JSM-IT510 Scanning Electron Microscope
operating at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV in Secondary Electron mode. For
each sample a contiguous set of 225 micrographs was captured using the Image
Montage function within the JEOL InScope software, operating a motorised stage,
applied parameters are tabulated (Table ~\ref{tab:microscopy_parameters}).
During acquisition, stigmation, contrast, and brightness were automatically
adjusted. A subsequent metadata analysis is detailed in the Supplementary
Information (Table ~\ref{tab:contrast-brightness-summary}), confirmed that

\subsection{Composite assembly}

The individual micrographs were assembled into a single composite, via an
enhanced phase correlation method, operated as a plugin of the Fiji software
package \citep{schindelin2012fiji, Preibisch2009}. The approach optimises
micrograph positions globally to sub-pixel accuracy and smooths the transitions
between micrographs by a non-linear intensity blending function.  The resulting
composites exhibit no visible delineation between individual micrographs. This
produces a total area comparable with previous OM studies of nuclear graphite,
and resolves channel porosity clearly (Figure \ref{fig:histogramandcomposite}
\citep{Huang2019,Kane2011a,huang2021statistical}). All composites were cropped
to a size of 1,350 µm $\times$ 950 µm.

For the construction of the histogram of intensity, where porosity is to be 
distinguished, per 8-bit micrograph with pixel values \(0 \le E(x,y) \le 255\) 
the intensity histogram \(H(i)\) is computed for \(i=0,1,\dots,255\) (Figure

\begin{equation}\tag{i}
H(i) = \sum_{x=1}^{M}\sum_{y=1}^{N} I\{E(x,y)=i\},

where \(M\) and \(N\) are the micrograph width and height (in pixels), and
\begin{equation}\tag{ii}
I\{E(x,y)\} =
\begin{cases}
I, & \text{if the pixel at }(x,y)\text{ has intensity }i,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The indicator function \(I\{E(x,y)\}\) increments the bin
corresponding to that greyscale value. The double sum
\(\sum_{x=1}^M\sum_{y=1}^N\) performs  across each pixel in the micrograph
resulting in a histogram \(H(i)\) of 256 bins, each bin representing number of
pixels in the micrograph at each greyscale value \(i\).

A global intensity threshold was applied to each composite micrograph to
generate a binary image, segmenting porosity and bulk.  Automatic global
thresholding algorithms available in Fiji were attempted but found to be
unsuitable for this dataset (see Supplementary Information). As with previous works,
threshold was determined manually from the resultant intensity histograms
\citep{Huang2019, Kane2011a, Taylor2016,huang2021statistical}.

Threshold selection was guided by two physical criteria. Firstly, for consistency
with the mercury porosimetry data by capturing the pore throats rather than the
contours of the pore bodies. Secondly, the exclusion of Closed Pore Volume (CPV)
incidentally exposed by sample preparation, which appear as brighter grey
regions. Both criteria were met by the application of a threshold which captures
the darkest regions in SEM micrographs, excluding CPV and capturing pore
throats. Nominally, this translated to an intensity threshold of $0.95 \pm 0.075$,
normalized between 0-1 over an 8-bit range.

	\subsubsection{Pore Diameter Thresholding}
  Following binarisation, a minimum pore size threshold was applied to
  distinguish genuine porosity from surface rugosity and imaging artefacts, as
  in previous OM based studies of nuclear graphite
  \citep{KANE2018369,Taylor2016} Threshold selection was informed by a
  sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Information).

  Based on the sensitivity analysis and inspection of the resulting
  segmentations (Figure \ref{fig:improvedporediademo})  a pore area threshold of
  2 µm² was determined to be the optimal compromise.

  This threshold minimised Type I errors (the incorrect classification of other
  features, as porosity) while minimising Type II errors (the erroneous
  exclusion of genuine pores). This threshold was applied across all samples.

\subsubsection{Channel Porosity Analysis and Calculation}
Following the application of the intensity and diameter thresholds, channel
porosity was quantified using the Analyse Particles function in Fiji. This
identifies all pixels surpassing the intensity threshold, generating connected
regions, pores, via an 8-connected (Moore neighbourhood) flood-fill algorithm.
Any two pixels that exceed the intensity threshold and are either directly
adjacent or diagonally connected are classified as belonging to the same region
(pore). The area and diameter per pore is calculated by converting the pixel
count into physical area. 

The result of this full workflow on a representative micrograph is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:C1-ig430f fused cropped 8 bit one color threshed subsample 2um
areathresh}. This analysis yields both a channel porosity (\%) and a channel
porosity size distribution which are presented in the Results section.

\subsection{Experimentally-Derived Dataset}

In conjunction with the SEM-based inputs to this multi-technique
characterisation, this study utilised existing experimental data for helium
pycnometry, mercury intrusion porosimetry, and gas adsorption. The dataset was
extracted from the comprehensive characterisation study of Jones \textit{et al} and
replicated for this study \citep{Jones2018}.

The samples analysed in that work were taken from the same parent blocks of
IG-110 and IG-430 as those prepared for the SEM analysis in this study to ensure
direct comparability. For the purposes of the modelling performed here, the key
parameters from the that work were adopted. For porosimetry, advancing and
receding contact angles of 140$^{\circ}$ and 130$^{\circ}$ and for pycnometry a
surface tension of 0.480 N m$^{-1}$ as well as a theoretical graphite density of
2.26 g cm$^{-3}$. Full experimental details for these techniques can be found in
the original publication \citep{Jones2018}.

\section{Modelling}

The modelling software PoreXpert proceeds inversely from effect, the
multi-technique percolation curve, to cause, a 3-dimensional void network. The
package constructs an 8-dimensional parameter space, composed of 5 numerical
parameters representing the physical characteristics of the pore network, and 3
constraining Boolean parameters. A Boltzmann-annealed amoeboid simplex searches
over this parameter space to find the set of parameters that produces a
percolation curve minimally different from the intrusion curve derived from
mercury porosimetry and gas adsorption.  The intrusion curve is extended to
smaller void sizes than Hg porosimetry could non-destructively probe, by GCMC
interpretation of gas adsorption (Figure \ref{fig:percolationcurve}) The
total intrusion data spans a total pore diameter range from 0.4 nm to 115 µm.
The intrusion curve is formed of experimental output from both methods, as
mercury porosimetry cannot accurately probe the smallest voids of interest due
to damage to the internal microstructure \cite{Jones2020}. The model output is a
simulated pore network with porosity and percolation properties matching the
experimental measurements (Figure \ref{fig:PX3dnetwork}).

  \caption{Example of a percolation curve generated by the PoreXpert software(WILL IMPROVE CAPTION)}

During modelling, the approximation type reflecting the depth of probing into
the porous structure is selected by the user (Figure \ref{fig:pxapproxtypes}).
Approximation Type 2 \textit{Surface connected throats and the visible volumes
of connected pores} was determined to be the optimal approximation for the
channel porosity dataset following examination of the micrographs indicating
that significant portions of the underlying pore bodies were captured during
segmentation.

  \label{fig:pxapproxtypes}

  Channel porosity (\%) per sample, as well as the minimum, and maximum pore
  diameters, 1.128 µm and 15 µm respectively, were input into the modelling
  software. The lower bound represents the minimum pore diameter threshold
  applied, while the upper pore diameter threshold represents the maximum pore
  diameter successfully captured by this method (Figure \ref{fig:IG110LogPSDs},
  \ref{fig:IG430LogPSDs}). These pore diameter bounds enable the  model to adjust
  the extent of the PSD which the channel porosity provides the input for, with
  porosity smaller and larger these bounds modelled via the experimentally
  derived data.

  Stochastic generations for multiple structure types were performed to identify
  the most representative structure type. For each sample, 10 stochastic
  generations were modelled, and the most representative sample identified using
  via sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Information). The chosen stochastic
  generation per sample was used to generate the final model outputs.

\section{Discussion}

Modelling outputs were highly congruent with experimentally derived open
porosity figures provided by the manufacturer for both IG-110 and IG-430,
providing confidence in the full scale multi-technique characterisation
approach here developed (Table ~\ref{tab:materialstable}, Table
~\ref{tab:Openporositytable}).

Huang \textit{et al.} ~\citep{huang2021statistical} found that channel porosity
as classified by SEM was 3\% for IG-110, which is in close agreement with the
range derived in this work. Interpolation via the adjustment of the
intensity threshold to match previous OM works (i.e., pore contours are
classified, as opposed to pore throats) produces results consistent with
those works. Kane \textit{et al.} derive a channel porosity of 14.73\% for
IG-110, with Huang and Tang deriving 14\% for the same grade, congruent with the
13-14\% channel porosity derived by this work following interpolation
\citep{Kane2011a, Huang2019}.


Nevertheless, artefacts exist in the segmentation process which limit the
  representativeness of the pore size distribution.

  The use of the Moore neighbourhood connectivity criterion, defining two or
  more pores connected by even a single adjacent pixel as one pore, agglomerates
  some proportion of the porosity. This would result in a rightward shift in the
  pore size distribution relative to the distribution which would be derived
  from an unagglomerated dataset. The use of a simple connectivity algorithm
  means that a single pore with an intersecting feature beneath the intensity
  threshold is fragmented into multiple pores, as some of these fragments fall
  below the pore diameter threshold and are discarded. This leads to an leftward
  shift of the pore size distribution. The extent to which pore fragmentation
  cancels out the effect of pore agglomeration was not here determined. 
  
  In future works, the use of a more sophisticated connectivity algorithm such
  as the watershed algorithm, may be able to reduce of agglomeration and
  fragmentation artefacts. A more complete solution would be to resin-impregnate
  the samples, as this would remove the need for the intensity thresholding
  stage altogether, although this was not here attempted as the intention of
  this work was to demonstrate the effectiveness of the multi-technique
  characterisation approach as it would be applied to heterogeneous samples,
  where the same sample must be characterised by all techniques, which resin
  impregnation would have precluded.

  \section{Conclusion}

This work has met the original objective in successfully demonstrating a
multi-technique characterisation approach the full pore size
distribution of nuclear grade graphite, from 0.4 nm to 115 µm. This approach
successfully generated a percolation curve that yields an open porosity value in
close agreement with the value determined experimentally by He pycnometry,  with
the modelled open porosity values for both IG-110 and IG-430 being within 0.56\%
and 0.71\% of the experimentally derived values respectively.

\subsection{Attempted Automatic Global Intensity Thresholding}

None of the 17 automatic global intensity thresholding algorithms available in
ImageJ were able to distinguish between porosity and bulk in this dataset,
(Figure ~\ref{fig:Try All Thresholding Methods}). For each sample, the histogram
\(H(i)\) produced by this method exhibited only a single peak, yielding no
statistically distinct intensity classes (Figure \ref{fig:histogramandcomposite}).
This is attributed to the small area fraction represented by porosity at the
operational magnification and resolution. This low fraction fails to yield a
secondary peak for the pore phase in the histogram. These algorithms operate by
identifying a threshold value between peaks and are therefore not functional for
this dataset.

  \subsection{Modelling Sensitivity Analysis}

  Sensitivity analysis consisted of assembling the five simplex parameters,
  distance between simulated and experimental percolation curves, and open
  porosity estimate into a single column per stochastic generation. Conditional
  formatting was applied to highlight those parameters within one standard
  deviation of the mean, with the stochastic generation with the lowest
  distance, closest open porosity to that measured by He pycnometry for that
  grade, and within one standard deviation of the mean, selected as the
  representative model.
